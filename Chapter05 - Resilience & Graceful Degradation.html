What happens when a
service goes down?
Every system fails eventually. The question isn't whether your dependencies break — it's whether your architecture survives when they do.
🔌
Circuit Breakers
Detect repeated failures and stop sending requests to a broken dependency. Like a fuse box for your services.
↩️
Fallback Strategies
When the ML ranker fails, serve a cached chronological feed. A degraded experience is far better than a blank screen.
🔄
Retries with Backoff
Retry transient failures — but with exponential delays. Naive retries at full speed turn a minor outage into a stampede.
🌊
Cascading Failures
One slow dependency can exhaust all your threads. Timeouts, bulkheads, and shedding load prevent a ripple from becoming a wave.
⚡
Real-world cost
A single missing timeout on a downstream call brought down a major social platform for 6 hours. The database was fine. The threads were not. Resilience patterns are not optional at scale.

⚠️
Root cause
The ML Ranker had 99.5% uptime — impressive on its own. But with 5 dependencies at 99.5% each, combined availability drops to 97.5%. Add no timeouts, and a single slow call blocks all feed threads until the whole service fails.
🎙
The Resilience Stack Solution
Three layered patterns that prevent a single failure from becoming a system-wide outage.
① Circuit Breaker — stop the bleeding
After N consecutive failures, the breaker opens: requests fail fast (without hitting the dependency) for a configurable window. After the window, it enters half-open — one probe request allowed. If that succeeds, the breaker closes again.
🟢
Closed
Normal traffic flows. Failures tracked. Under threshold.
🔴
Open
Failing fast. Zero requests reach broken dependency.
🟡
Half-Open
One probe sent. If it passes, reset to Closed.
② Fallback — degrade gracefully, not completely
When the circuit is open, don't return an error. Return something useful instead. ML ranker down? Serve a cached chronological feed. Notification service down? Show last known unread count. User preference service down? Use sensible defaults. A degraded experience is nearly always better than a blank screen or a 500 error.
③ Retries with Exponential Backoff — don't stampede
Retry transient failures — but wait longer each time: 100ms → 200ms → 400ms → 800ms, plus a random jitter to prevent synchronised retry waves. Add a deadline budget: if the original request has < 200ms remaining, skip the retry entirely. Never retry non-idempotent writes without deduplication.

Key Concepts Summary
The resilience toolkit every production system needs.
🔌
Circuit Breaker
Three states: Closed (normal), Open (fail fast), Half-Open (probe). Configured with failure threshold (e.g. 5 failures in 10s) and recovery window (e.g. 30s). Prevents thread exhaustion from a slow dependency.
↩️
Graceful Fallback
Every critical dependency should have a fallback. Cached response, default value, or simplified feature. The fallback tier should be stateless and fast — it runs when things are already stressed.
⏱️
Timeouts + Budgets
Every outbound call must have a hard timeout. Use deadline propagation: if the parent request expires in 80ms, cap all child calls to 60ms total. A missing timeout is a thread leak waiting to happen.
🧱
Bulkhead Isolation
Give each downstream dependency its own thread pool or connection pool. If the ML ranker exhausts its pool, the Redis and notification pools are unaffected. Blast radius is contained.
💡
The rule of thumb
Design for the failure of every dependency — not just catastrophic crashes, but slow tails too. A 99.5% reliable service looks great in isolation. With five of them in a call chain with no protection, your system is only 97.5% reliable. Add circuit breakers, timeouts, and fallbacks. Users should never see a blank screen because an internal scoring model had a bad deploy.
